# Practice tests, drills, and flashcards

Read this before writing any question-shaped artifact.

## Match the real format, not a generic one

Build the practice in the shape of the actual paper.

- An MCQ exam gets MCQ practice with real distractors. Not open prompts,
  not "explain in your own words."
- A problem-set exam gets full problems worked to the teacher's own notation and
  rounding.
- An essay exam gets rubric rows, not content summaries.
- A mixed paper gets each section in its own format and its own timing.

**An announced format usually names one section.** "Mostly multiple choice"
often means multiple choice *plus* a long problem worth a third of the marks.
Ask, and if unanswered, build both and label the assumption.

## Build every test from its own course only

Use that course's past papers, that teacher's materials, that subject's notes
and announcements, and the student's own results in that subject.

**Do not open another subject's practice test as a template** - not for wording,
item style, explanation style, structure, difficulty, or item count, not even
"just for format." Every teacher tests differently, so borrowed shape is
borrowed error.

## Distractor design

An item whose wrong options are obviously wrong tests nothing. Build each
distractor from a real, specific error:

- The near-synonym the student confuses with the right term.
- The result of the common procedural slip - the sign flipped, the step skipped,
  the wrong base used.
- The value from the *previous* step, which looks like an answer.
- The textbook's answer where the teacher's method gives a different one.
- The plausible-sounding item that is out of scope.

## Answer keys teach the trap

Every item's explanation covers:

1. Why the right answer is right, in one line.
2. **Why each wrong option was tempting** - the specific error or confusion it
   represents.
3. What to watch for when this pattern appears again.

A key that gives only the letter has taught nothing. This is where most of the
learning actually happens, so it gets the most words.

## Mixing in prior material

Many teachers chain topics - a question on the current chapter that silently
requires a method from three chapters back. If past papers show that pattern, do
not scope the practice to only the named chapter. Include the chained
prerequisites and say why they are there.

## Timing

Give the real duration and show elapsed time. A timer should never auto-submit
or lock the test - running past time and finishing is more useful than a
truncated attempt, as long as the overtime is visible.

# Flashcards

Cards exist to be graded by the student as recalled / not recalled, then
scheduled. That constrains what a card can be.

- **One idea per card.** A paragraph cannot be graded, so it cannot be
  scheduled.
- **The front is a complete question or a standalone term.** A fragment that
  only makes sense under a column header becomes gibberish on its own.
- **The back is short and checkable.** If the student cannot tell whether they
  got it right, the card is broken.
- **Do not auto-generate cards from every table in a note.** Make them when
  asked. A deck full of half-relevant cards makes the review queue worthless.
- Cards whose answers are placeholders are noise. Leave them out.

Spacing intervals that work in practice: 0, 1, 3, 7, 21 days, promoting on a
correct recall and resetting on a miss.
